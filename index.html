Quick vocabulary lesson, because the internet still gets this wrong. Hemp and marijuana are the same species, Cannabis sativa L., bred for different jobs, the same way a draft horse and a Thoroughbred are both horses (Small, 2015). The legal line is THC. Hemp is cannabis with no more than 0.3% THC on a dry weight basis (Johnson, 2018), and starting 11 December 2026 that number counts total THC, including THCA, the acid form the plant actually makes (Neuhofer, 2026). See that sneaky hemp sample? Legal hemp under the old rule, marijuana under the new one. Anything above the line is marijuana. It’s still Schedule I federally, but California licenses it, which matters later.

So why should investors care about a plant with an identity crisis? Because it’s ridiculously versatile. The Congressional Research Service counts more than 25,000 hemp products across nine market segments, from car door panels and paper to building materials, food, and skin care (Johnson, 2018). And it uses the whole plant. The stalk gives you fiber and the woody core called hurd. The seed gives you oil and protein. The flower gives you cannabinoids like CBD. Most companies pick one of those and leave the rest in the field, even though the research says hemp pays best when you use all of it (Pal & Lucia, 2019). Hold that thought, I’m coming back to it.

Here’s the part that matters most for the science. A variety bred for fiber is a terrible place to look for CBD, and the reverse is just as true. Fiber hemp like Futura 75 is tall and skinny and puts its energy into the stalk (Salentijn et al., 2015). Grain hemp is short, early, and loaded with seed, so pollination is actually the goal there. CBD hemp and THC-dominant cannabis both grow for resin, which is made in glandular trichomes on the unpollinated female flowers (Andre et al., 2016). Whether a plant leans CBD or THC, its chemotype, comes down to genetics (Small, 2015). So every product gets a plant built for it, instead of one crop doing four jobs badly.

[Ask the room to call each choice, or pick a couple wrong on purpose for the laughs, hit Grow, then “Show me the right way.”]

Four products means four completely different ways to grow, and getting one wrong ruins the crop. Dry Humor gets 150 acres on our leased farm near Gilroy, seeded thick so the plants crowd each other and grow tall instead of bushy, and cut at early flowering (Amaducci et al., 2015). Grain Damage gets 75 acres, and here pollen is the whole point, because pollinated flowers turn into seed. Spritz Happens is the opposite. It gets two acres in a light-deprivation greenhouse, grown only from clones of one tested mother, and male plants are banned, because a pollinated flower stops making resin and starts making seeds (Andre et al., 2016). That’s 227 acres, under the county’s 250-acre cap (County of Santa Clara Division of Agriculture, n.d.). And the recreational crop grows indoors in licensed space in San José, frozen fresh at harvest so the terpenes survive. Same species, four rulebooks.

CBD extraction · run the lab

Making Spritz Happens

Andre et al. (2016); Pal & Lucia (2019)

Bad Seed Botanicals · 7

[Work through all six stations live. “Skip station” moves on if you’re short on time.]

Here’s how a flower turns into Spritz Happens. Station one, we harvest only the flowers, because that’s where the trichomes are. And the plant doesn’t actually make CBD. It makes CBDA, using an enzyme called CBDA synthase inside those trichomes (Andre et al., 2016). Station two, supercritical CO2 extraction. CO2 goes supercritical above 31 degrees Celsius and 74 bar, and of the three common methods it’s rated the safest and highest-yielding (Pal & Lucia, 2019). The catch is that waxes and fats come along for the ride, so station three, we winterize: chill it and filter the wax out. Station four, gentle heat knocks a carboxyl group off as CO2 and turns CBDA into CBD. Too hot and you cook off the terpenes. Station five, we distill and pull the THC down to non-detect. And station six, it goes into MCT oil with the plant’s own terpenes in a metered spray. A mouth spray absorbs faster than a gummy, and nobody has to guess their dose.

[Do the water test first so it soaks in, then three coats, then the water test again so it beads up. Watch the microscope change each coat.]

Grain Damage is where hemp seed oil finally gets a real job. Hemp seed is about 30% oil, and roughly 80% of that is polyunsaturated fat, mostly linoleic and alpha-linolenic acid at about a three to one ratio (Callaway, 2004). Watch the microscope. Bare wood is a bunch of open, thirsty cells, so water soaks right in. Coat one, the oil wicks into those cells, and every one of those long fatty acid chains is carrying carbon double bonds. Coat two, oxygen from the air attacks those double bonds and the chains start grabbing onto each other. Coat three, they’re cross-linked into a tough network inside the grain, and now water beads up and rolls off. It’s the same chemistry that makes the bottle in your pantry go rancid, just pointed in a useful direction. And pop quiz: no, this oil is not CBD oil. Seeds carry trace cannabinoids at best.

Fiber and textiles · run the mill

Making Dry Humor

Amaducci et al. (2015); Pal & Lucia (2019); Salentijn et al. (2015)

Bad Seed Botanicals · 9

[Pick the wrong harvest time once for the joke, then early flowering. Crank, sort a few pieces, blend, weave.]

Dry Humor starts in the field, and timing matters a lot. We cut at early flowering, when yield and quality balance out best (Amaducci et al., 2015). Wait longer and the plant builds secondary bast fiber, which is shorter and coarser (Salentijn et al., 2015), and now your towel exfoliates you without asking. Gilroy summers are too dry for traditional dew retting, so we decorticate mechanically right on the farm. That cracks the stalk into two things. The long outer bast fiber is the textile part. The woody inner core is hurd. Modern decortication gets roughly three tons of bast and seven tons of hurd per hectare (Pal & Lucia, 2019). The bast gets cottonized and blended with cotton, because hemp brings the strength and absorbency and cotton brings the softness. Then a partner mill weaves the terry. The hurd becomes animal bedding, which, as an animal science major, I’m contractually obligated to mention.

[Tap the top card to toss it to the back as you go from federal down to city.]

Now the rules, because in this industry, where you are is half the business plan. Federally, the new hemp definition kicks in on 11 December. It counts total THC including THCA, and it caps finished hemp cannabinoid products at 0.4 milligrams of THC per container (Neuhofer, 2026). The FDA also still gets a say in how CBD is sold (Mead, 2019). In California, AB 8 sends anything with intoxicating cannabinoids through licensed cannabis retail, hemp-derived or not (Cannabis: Cannabinoids: Industrial Hemp, 2025). That’s a headache for a lot of hemp brands. For us it’s convenient, since our recreational product was already headed there. Locally, the county caps hemp at 250 acres (County of Santa Clara Division of Agriculture, n.d.), and San José isn’t taking new cultivation registrations (City of San José Division of Cannabis Regulation, n.d.), so we partner with or buy an operator that already has one.

So what do customers actually get? Every product comes from a plant bred for that job, and we control it from seed to shelf. Someone buying Spritz Happens knows what’s in the bottle because we grew it, extracted it, and tested it ourselves. And here’s the part I like best. Look at what most companies throw away. When we decorticate the stalk for Dry Humor, the woody hurd that’s left over becomes absorbent bedding for horses and poultry. When we press the seed for Grain Damage, the leftover seed cake is high in protein, so it’s headed for livestock feed once the feed approvals catch up. And the used bedding composts right back into the field we grow in. The lab isn’t done either: a CBG-forward spray and a faster-curing Grain Damage are next. Being the animal science person, I’ll admit the bedding is my favorite.

Here’s the market. U.S. hemp production was worth 739 million dollars in 2025, up 64% from the year before (U.S. Department of Agriculture, National Agricultural Statistics Service, 2026). Most of that value is floral hemp, which is exactly the part of the market the new federal definition goes after in December (Neuhofer, 2026). Fiber went the other way. Acreage grew, but the value of raw fiber dropped, because a bale of stalks is a commodity and anyone with a field can grow one. A finished towel is a different story. Most companies in this space run one lane, a CBD brand or a seed food brand or a fiber processor. That’s one revenue stream and one regulatory risk, and a lot of them are about to find out what that feels like.

Every hemp company has problems. Here’s how we handled ours. THC drift in the CBD crop gets tested clones and pre-harvest testing, with an early cut if the numbers start climbing. The dry Gilroy summer that rules out dew retting gets on-farm decortication. Seed oil’s habit of going rancid gets fast drying and low-oxygen packaging. And the regulatory squeeze gets THC taken to non-detect, plus a licensed retail channel we were going to need anyway. Our strategy is simple. Four products from one species, two regulatory lanes, and nothing left in the field. When one market dips, the other three keep paying. Cannabis has been misunderstood for a long time, and we just think it’s time it got put to work properly. Thank you, and I’m happy to take questions.
